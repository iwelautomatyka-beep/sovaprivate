How Sova is secured

In one paragraph

Your phones share secret keys that no server of ours ever receives. Pairing hands them over without the PIN ever crossing the network. At home, the phones talk directly, encrypted end to end. Away from home, alerts, sound and video pass through our server encrypted with keys only your phones have, which is why we can forward them and cannot read them.

This page explains how Sova protects what it carries, in enough detail to judge the claims in the privacy policy. It describes the version of the app currently on Google Play. A full technical specification is available to independent security reviewers on request.

Pairing, without ever sending the PIN

A parent phone joins by scanning the code on the nursery phone or typing its four-digit PIN. The PIN is never sent: both phones prove they know it with a password-authenticated key exchange (SRP-6a), so a device listening on your Wi-Fi learns nothing it could use later. Pairing works only on the home Wi-Fi and only while the nursery phone is showing its code. The nursery limits wrong attempts and replaces the PIN when someone keeps guessing.

A correct PIN is still not enough: the nursery phone shows who is asking and waits for the person holding it to tap Let in. Only then does the new phone receive the keys of the room, and a key of its own that only the nursery and that phone know.

What our server gets, and what it does not

Our server never receives the keys that encrypt your alerts, sound or video. It gets one-way verification keys derived from them, enough to check that a request really comes from one of your phones, and useless for reading anything. These verification keys are symmetric, and we say so plainly: someone who took over our server could send requests as if from your phones, but still could not read what passes through it.

At home: the two phones talk directly

On your Wi-Fi the phones find each other and connect directly, without our server. Each connection starts with a fresh key agreement (elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman), authenticated with the phone's own key, and everything after it is encrypted with AES-256-GCM. A device on your Wi-Fi can see that two phones are talking, and nothing more. The nursery announces itself on the network with a code that changes every hour; its name appears only while the pairing card is open.

Away from home: through us, but closed to us

Alerts, the nursery's status, sound and video are encrypted on the phone before they leave it, with keys our server does not have. Google, which delivers the notifications, sees only that something was sent and when. Sound and video through the relay use a session key agreed between the phones for each session and never stored, so a recording of today's traffic cannot be opened later even if a key leaked. Every message carries protection against being replayed.

Removing a phone

Each parent phone has its own key. Disconnecting one on the nursery cuts it off at home and on our server at once, and the nursery gives the remaining phones a new shared key, sealed separately for each of them, so the disconnected phone cannot read anything new. The other phones carry on without pairing again. A new code on the nursery starts the room from scratch.

What this does not protect you from

An honest list is worth more than a long one.

  • Someone holding an unlocked phone. Either phone in the pair can see and hear the nursery. The app's security begins where the phone's own lock ends.
  • Someone who can read the nursery's screen and reach the nursery phone. The PIN and code are on it, on purpose. Pairing also needs your Wi-Fi and the consent of whoever is holding the nursery phone; if a phone was let in by mistake, disconnect it or give the nursery a new code.
  • Us, if you stop trusting the app itself. Everything above is done by code we wrote and ship. It means our server cannot read your stream; it does not mean an app can prove its own good behaviour.
  • Traffic analysis. We can see that a room is active, when the nursery sends something, and how much data moves through it. We cannot see what is in it.
